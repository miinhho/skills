# Formulaic prose: patterns and exceptions

Use these distinctions when composing, not only when editing. A pattern is evidence to inspect, not proof of AI authorship or an automatic deletion rule. Consider what the sentence contributes and how often its shape recurs across the whole piece. Examples below are invented.

## Inflated significance and authority

Problem: “이번 변경은 업무 방식을 혁신적으로 바꾸는 중요한 전환점입니다.” No change is actually described.

If the supplied fact is that staff can approve a request in its detail screen, write: “이제 요청 상세 화면에서 바로 승인할 수 있습니다.” Do not add time saved or universal user benefits without evidence.

Keep a strong conclusion when evidence supports it. Replace “experts agree” with an attributable, relevant source or omit the unsupported authority. Merely adding a citation does not justify a stronger claim than that source makes.

## Empty contrasts

Problem: “이 안내는 단순한 정보가 아니라 새로운 가능성의 시작입니다.” The negative half answers no real misconception; the positive half supplies no detail.

State the actual purpose: “이 안내는 실험실 장비를 처음 예약하는 사람을 위한 것입니다.”

Keep: “예약은 장비 사용을 보장하지만, 교육 이수를 대신하지는 않습니다.” Both halves distinguish real requirements. Do not ban “not X but Y,” “rather than,” or their Korean equivalents as strings.

## Announcements and declarations of importance

Problem: “핵심은 분명합니다. 지금부터 그 의미를 자세히 살펴보겠습니다.”

Usually proceed directly to the point. Keep a short roadmap when it helps navigate a long explanation or a complex procedure. A transition that establishes an actual dependency is not filler.

## Repeated closers and manufactured lessons

Problem: each section ends with “이것이 중요한 이유입니다,” followed by a final summary that repeats all the section summaries.

Remove the echo. A conclusion can instead draw a supported implication, state a decision, reconcile the evidence, or identify what remains unresolved. Do not force a personal anecdote to become a universal lesson.

Keep a concise recap when the text is long enough that it helps readers retain or act on the substance. Its function matters more than whether its words appeared earlier.

## Forced parallelism and formatting

Problem: inventing a weak third point to make every list a triad; putting a bold label before every sentence; making every section follow the same opening-example-moral mold.

Let the material determine grouping and length. Use a table when the reader compares shared dimensions, a list for parallel choices or steps, and connected prose for reasoning. Keep deliberate parallel phrasing that makes genuine alternatives easier to compare.

## Abstract decoration and vague references

Problem: “이를 통해 가치의 밀도를 높이고 새로운 가능성의 지평을 엽니다.” Neither the reference nor the consequence is identifiable.

Name the mechanism and consequence supported by the source. If the source does not establish one, do not invent it. A clear smaller claim is better than a vivid unsupported one. Metaphors are useful when they explain a relationship; they need not be removed because they are figurative.

## Process residue and defensive filler

Problem: inserting “I reviewed the materials carefully,” “this is not a claim of universal effectiveness,” or “no attachment was provided” into an article that does not need those statements.

Write what the reader needs from the investigation. Keep an evidence limitation when it changes interpretation, and disclose missing information when a required field or material decision depends on it. Do not invent an exaggerated claim merely to disclaim it.

## Overcorrection

Removing stock phrasing is not permission to add slang, anger, opinions, fabricated lived experience, or abrupt fragments. Do not make every sentence equally short. Preserve useful repetition, measured uncertainty, and the author's actual temperament. A well-written passage can remain unchanged.
